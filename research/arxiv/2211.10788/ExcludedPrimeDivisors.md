# Primes excluded from the Lucas–Fibonacci sums

Let `S(n) = 2Fₙ + Fₙ₊₂`. Every prime in the classes **13, 17, 21, 29 and 41 modulo 44** divides no `S(n)`. Each class contains infinitely many such primes. This is an unconditional partial result for the third diagonal of the determinant Hosoya triangle. The upstream conjecture that infinitely many primes *equal* some `S(n)` remains open. [Lean proof](LucasFibonacciExcludedPrimes.lean) · [pinned upstream definition and conjecture](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/f606af160664c5a10952e9a957b100a1cd45bbeb/FormalConjectures/Arxiv/2211.10788/LucasFibonacciSumPrimes.lean) · [Ching et al., introduction and §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.10788)

Write `a = S(n)` and `b = S(n+1)`. The recurrence sends `(a,b)` to `(b,a+b)`, which reverses the sign of `b² − ab − a²`. The initial pair `(1,4)` gives 11. Thus if `p ∣ S(n)`, then `11 ≡ (−1)ⁿ S(n+1)² (mod p)`. When `p ≡ 1 (mod 4)`, the right side is a square modulo `p`. Quadratic reciprocity forces `p` to be a square modulo 11. The five classes listed above are precisely the residues congruent to 1 modulo 4 and nonsquare modulo 11. Dirichlet's theorem gives infinitely many primes in each.

The sequence is the tail starting at index 1 of the Lagarias sequence with initial pair `(3,1)`. Moree and Stevenhagen studied its prime-divisor density under GRH and derived a related quadratic restriction in §4, equation (4.4), for primes inert in their quadratic field. The Lean argument here uses the recurrence and quadratic reciprocity for every prime congruent to 1 modulo 4. [Moree and Stevenhagen, *Prime divisors of the Lagarias sequence*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9912253)
