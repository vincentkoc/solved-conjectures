# Quadratic seeds for polynomial Cunningham chains

For every positive length `k` and either recurrence `g ↦ Xg ± 1`, infinitely many quadratic polynomials `f ∈ ℤ[X]` with positive leading coefficient have their first `k` terms irreducible over ℚ and their term at index `k` reducible. Index zero denotes the seed. This proves the finite-length polynomial analogue in Jones’s Corollary 1.5. Jones published a corrigendum to the original proof in 2012. The proof here constructs two quadratic families and establishes their irreducibility directly. [Lean proof](CunninghamPolynomialLengths.lean) · [Jones, Corollary 1.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.1579#page=2) · [Jones’s corrigendum](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2012.02.015)

| Kind | Seed | Free parameter | Value of term `i` at 1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First, `Xg+1` | `X² − (k+1+c)X + c` | Any integer `c ≥ 1` | `i − k` |
| Second, `Xg−1` | `pX² − (p−k−1)X − 1` | Any prime `p > k+1` | `k − i` |

At index `k` the factor `X−1` divides a polynomial of degree `k+2` in both families. Varying the constant coefficient in the first family and the leading coefficient in the second gives infinitely many distinct seeds. The theorem imposes no conditions on later terms.

**First-kind irreducibility.** At an index `i < k`, the polynomial is monic, takes a negative value at 1, and has nonnegative coefficients below its subleading term. Take a real root `r > 1` and divide by `X−r`. Coefficient comparison from the constant term upwards shows that every nonleading coefficient of the monic quotient is nonpositive. Its value at 1 is positive, so the sum of the absolute values of its lower coefficients is less than one. All its roots are strictly inside the unit circle. Any nonconstant integral monic factor with nonzero constant term must have at least one root on or outside the circle, by the product of its roots. Only the linear factor can supply such a root. Gauss’s lemma gives irreducibility over ℚ.

**Second-kind irreducibility.** The leading coefficient is the prime `p`, the constant term is −1, and all nonleading coefficients are nonpositive. The positive value at 1 makes the leading coefficient larger than the sum of the absolute values of all other coefficients. Thus all roots lie strictly inside the unit circle. In an integral factorization one factor must have leading coefficient ±1. The product of its roots forces that factor to be constant, and the constant term of the original polynomial forces it to be a unit. Again apply Gauss’s lemma.

**Comparison with the integer conjecture.** If `f(2) = p ≥ 1`, evaluation at 2 gives Jones’s pinned integer recurrence term by term. Polynomial factorization still gives a different stopping condition. For the second-kind seed `17X²−15X−1`, the first successor factors as `(X−1)(17X²+2X+1)`. The seed and successor evaluate at 2 to the primes 37 and 73. The polynomial chain has exact length one, while 37 does not start a second-kind integer chain of length one. Infinitude of the integer chains at fixed lengths `k ≥ 2` remains open.
