# Prime-power block sizes at every sufficiently large admissible order

Fix a prime power $k$. For every sufficiently large $n$, there is a family of $k$-element subsets of $\mathrm{Fin}\,n$ covering every pair exactly once if and only if $\binom{k-i}{2-i}$ divides $\binom{n-i}{2-i}$ for $i=0,1$. The cutoff depends on $k$. For $2<k$, the forward construction has exactly the type obtained by specializing the pinned Erdős 722 statement to rank two. The Lean theorem also includes $k=2$. [Spectrum and host implication](Erdos722PrimePowerBlockSpectrum.lean) · [Pinned statement](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/f606af160664c5a10952e9a957b100a1cd45bbeb/FormalConjectures/ErdosProblems/722.lean)

The earlier prime-power theorem required the order to be a prime power as well. For example, take $k=9$ and $n=225+360j$. Every such order is divisible by both three and five and satisfies the two tests, since $n\equiv9\pmod{72}$. The present result gives a design at every sufficiently large member of this progression. Wilson proved the classical pair-design existence theorem for all fixed block sizes, and Keevash proved the general rank theorem. This notebook formalizes the prime-power block-size case. ## Constructing arrays at every large order

For a fixed number $b\ge2$ of columns, let $s=(b!)^b-1$. Write the order as $v=st+r$, with $0<r\le t$. To choose $t$, the Chinese remainder theorem moves $\lfloor v/(s+1)\rfloor$ upward by a bounded amount. At each prime $p\le s$ prescribe $t\equiv1\pmod{p^b}$ if $p\mid v$, and $t\equiv-v\pmod{p^b}$ otherwise. Then $t$ is coprime to $s!$. If a prime $p\le b$ divides $r$, it cannot divide $v$, and $p^b$ divides both $(s+1)t$ and $t+v$. Their difference is $r$. Hence every prime-power factor occurring to its full multiplicity in $r$ has size at least $b$. [CRT and size inequalities](Erdos722ArrayArithmetic.lean)

These conditions supply unit-difference directions at the four component sizes $s,s+1,t,r$. A truncated transversal gives a pairwise balanced design with these block sizes. On each block put an array containing every constant row. Entries with different symbols determine a unique master block. Equal symbols determine the constant row, so local arrays agree wherever blocks meet. This gives a diagonal orthogonal array with $b$ columns on every sufficiently large alphabet. The classical asymptotic existence of arbitrarily many mutually orthogonal Latin squares is due to Chowla, Erdős and Straus. [Glue and coordinate construction](Erdos722DiagonalArrays.lean) · [Eventual arrays](Erdos722EventualArrays.lean) · [Chowla–Erdős–Straus](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1960-017-2)

## From affine designs to the two admissible residues

Write $n=(k-1)u+1$. The two binomial tests imply $k\mid nu$, and $n$ and $u$ are coprime. Since $k$ is a prime power, $k\mid u$ or $k\mid n$. The second case gives $u\equiv1\pmod k$ from $n+u=ku+1$. Both $u=0$ and $u=1$ have seed designs. Call $u$ usable when there is a pair design with $k$ points per block at this order. [Arithmetic and necessity](Erdos722PrimePowerBlockSpectrum.lean)

Let $m=(k^e-1)/(k-1)$. Affine lines over the field of order $k$ give a design at $k^e$. For large $e$, an array with $k$ columns at height $k^e-1$ lets us fill every column with a design using a common extra point. This gives the order $k(k^e-1)+1$, with group count $km$. At height $k^e$, fill the columns independently to obtain order $k^{e+1}$ and group count $km+1$. In the next dimension the parameter $m$ becomes $c=km+1$. Both pairs of adjacent counts $(km,km+1)$ and $(kc,kc+1)$ are therefore available. The same construction supplies arbitrarily large adjacent heights supporting arrays of any fixed width. [Truncation and filling](Erdos722ArrayTruncation.lean) · [Adjacent ingredients](Erdos722AffineIngredients.lean)

Using the two widths and choosing independently between adjacent heights gives displacements $km i+kc j$. The identity $c=km+1$ gives $\gcd(m,c)=1$. The two-generator numerical semigroup theorem now supplies long finite intervals of usable counts in both residues $0,1\pmod k$. These intervals can be dilated by $c$ or by $d=kc+1$. Rounding the full-column height so its one-point completion is divisible by $k$ preserves the target residue in the truncated column. A fixed margin absorbs the rounding error. Finally $\gcd(c,d)=1$, and both bases exceed one. Normalizing powers of $d$ by their leading powers of $c$ gives two close distinct products. Their overlapping interval translates cover a tail.
