# Green 28 with one fixed relabelling

Green asks whether two independent integer laws must each be uniform on their own support whenever their sum is uniform. It suffices to test **one law** $R$. Draw two independent samples from $R$, relabel the second with the same explicit integer involution $\tau$ in every test, and add them. The test may assume the sum is uniform on exactly $k^2$ outcomes, that $R$ has largest atom $1/k$, and that its support lies in a single block $[r^3,r^3+r^2)$. The test conclusion is that $R$ is uniform. Neither the radix nor the number of outcomes is bounded. ## The involution and the square

For each positive integer $r$, write sites in the block as $r^3+i+rj$ with $0\le i,j<r$. The map sends this integer to $r^3+j+ri$. It fixes every integer outside these blocks. The integer cube root recovers the radix because $r^3+r^2\le(r+1)^3$. Thus one map works for every support. [`transpose_pack` and `transpose_involutive`](Green28DigitTranspose.lean)

Suppose $P,Q$ have uniform sum on a finite set $S$ of size $k$. Choose $N$ bounding the absolute values of the occupied sites of both marginals and take $r=4N+1$. Form $R$ by sending an independent pair $(x,y)$ of law $P\otimes Q$ to $r^3+(x+N)+r(y+N)$. The occupied labels are distinct. Relabelling exchanges the two digits, giving the law encoded from $Q\otimes P$. Adding independent draws now produces two independent coordinate sums, each with the original uniform law on $S$. Their shifted digits range from zero through $4N$, so all $k^2$ ordered pairs of outcomes have different labels and mass $1/k^2$. [`SquareLift` and `exists_square_lift`](Green28DigitTranspose.lean)

Each mass of $R$ is a cell probability $P(x)Q(y)$. It is at most $1/k$ because that cell contributes to the original outcome $x+y$. The rightmost occupied sites make a sum with a single representation, so the bound is attained. A uniform law on the labelled product makes all cell probabilities equal. Fixing either coordinate then gives constant weights along the other. Conversely, uniform inputs give the uniform product law. The construction exposes the injective labelling and equality of the actual laws. [Product and peak-mass proofs](Green28DigitTranspose.lean)

For example, take uniform inputs on $\{-1,0\}$ and $\{0,2\}$. Their four sums are distinct. At radix 9 the new support is $\{748,749,766,767\}$, and its relabelled copy has support $\{740,742,749,751\}$. The sixteen pair sums are distinct. This also illustrates why the two samples must be independent even though their starting law is the same.

## Scope and sources

This is an equivalence for the unrestricted question. The forward implication applies the pinned assertion directly to $R$ and the pushforward $\tau_*R$. The converse transports a uniform sum and recovers both original marginals. The argument uses ordinary independent products and carry-free integer encoding.
